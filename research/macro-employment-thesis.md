The single exception was the 1960 recession.\n\n### The Mechanism: Reallocation, Not Inflation\n\nThe academic literature strongly supports the \"employment, not inflation\" thesis through two key channels:\n\n**Channel 1 -- Sectoral Reallocation (Hamilton's primary mechanism):**\nHamilton built models showing that when oil prices spike, resources must relocate from energy-intensive sectors (motor vehicles, chemicals, airlines) to less energy-dependent ones. But **labor and capital cannot move costlessly**. Workers specialized in auto manufacturing do not seamlessly become oil field workers. The result is **idle resources -- involuntary unemployment** that significantly multiplies the initial shock beyond energy's share of GDP. Hamilton shows that workers in depressed sectors wait for conditions to improve in their sector rather than shifting to favorable sectors, creating a persistent unemployment overhang.\n\n**Channel 2 -- Consumer Spending Diversion:**\nWhen energy costs spike, consumers who try to maintain real energy purchases must cut spending on other goods or reduce saving. Hamilton documented sharp asymmetry: when oil prices rise, consumers postpone car purchases; when prices fall, they do not buy a second car. The spending reduction cascades through non-energy sectors, destroying jobs there.\n\n**Key quantitative finding:** Oil shocks account for **20-25% of the cyclical variability in employment growth** -- twice as much as monetary policy shocks. Job reallocation rose by **11% of employment over 3-4 years** following the 1973 oil shock (Davis & Haltiwanger).\n\n### The Bernanke Debate\n\nBernanke, Gertler, and Watson (1997) argued that much of the employment decline was actually caused by the Fed's interest rate response to oil-driven inflation -- i.e., the monetary policy tightening. Hamilton pushed back, arguing the demand destruction and reallocation channels operate independently of monetary policy. The consensus has settled closer to Hamilton: the reallocation/demand-destruction channel is primary, with monetary tightening as an amplifier.\n\n### Key Papers\n- Hamilton, \"Oil and the Macroeconomy since World War II\" (1983)\n- Hamilton, \"What Is an Oil Shock?\" (2003)\n- Hamilton, \"Causes and Consequences of the Oil Shock of 2007-08\" (Brookings, 2009)\n- Hamilton, \"Historical Oil Shocks\" (NBER WP 16790, 2011)\n- Davis & Haltiwanger, \"Sectoral Job Creation and Destruction Responses to Oil Price Changes\" (2001)\n\nSources:\n- [Hamilton - Oil and the Macroeconomy (Palgrave, 2005)](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jhamilto/JDH_palgrave_oil.pdf)\n- [Hamilton - Historical Oil Shocks (NBER)](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w16790/w16790.pdf)\n- [Hamilton - Causes and Consequences of the Oil Shock of 2007-08 (Brookings)](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/2009a_bpea_hamilton-1.pdf)\n- [IMF - Oil Shocks and Labor Market Developments (2025)](https://www.imf.org/-/media/Files/Publications/WP/2025/English/WPIEA2025145.ashx)\n- [Herrera et al. - Oil Price Shocks and U.S. Economic Activity](https://gattonweb.uky.edu/faculty/herrera/documents/HKR_JEPO.pdf)\n\n---\n\n## 2.
