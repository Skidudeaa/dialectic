# Strait of Hormuz Closure — Updated Trading Analysis

*Revised June 3, 2026. This is a restructured, fact-checked version of the energy/Hormuz thesis from the source newsletter, with current market data substituted for the stale May figures and the editorial/ideological framing removed. Substantive analytical claims are preserved or corrected on the evidence; opinion is labeled as such. Not investment advice — see note at the end.*

---

## 1. Where the situation actually stands

The original piece was written in early-to-mid May. The facts have moved, and an honest update has to lead with that.

- The Strait has been **effectively closed to commercial shipping for roughly three months**, following the late-February strikes on Iran. As of June 3 it is running at a fraction of normal throughput — on the order of **~10 transits per day against a peacetime norm near 95**. Roughly 20% of seaborne oil and ~20% of global LNG normally move through it.
- The IEA has characterized this as **the largest oil supply disruption in the history of the global market**, larger than the 1970s shocks. The EIA's May Short-Term Energy Outlook estimated Gulf producers shut in on the order of **10.5 million bbl/day** in April, and the UAE formally left OPEC effective May 1, which cuts the cartel's usable spare capacity.
- **Price action:** Brent ran from a January low in the low-$60s to a mid-May peak around **$116–117**, then retraced roughly **15%** to sit near **$97–101** as of June 3. So the move is real and large (Brent is up ~50% year-on-year), but it is *not* a one-way line — the market is actively repricing the odds of resolution.
- **LNG:** QatarEnergy declared force majeure; Europe sources ~12–14% of its LNG from Qatar, essentially all of it via Hormuz.
- **Diplomacy:** reopening talks are ongoing but unresolved. Political statements that the Strait is "on the path to reopening" have not yet translated into shipowners returning — insurers and carriers are still treating it as unsafe. That gap between political optimism and commercial reality is the single most important thing to watch.

The base case from independent analysts (e.g., Wood Mackenzie) is supply tightness persisting through Q3 and a **shallow global recession in H2 2026** if the disruption drags on.

## 2. The core thesis: disruption likely lasts longer than consensus prices

The most defensible part of the original argument is this: **"open or closed" is a false binary.** The Red Sea is the live precedent — it is nominally open yet has operated far below capacity for an extended period because carriers and insurers won't accept the risk. Hormuz is showing the same pattern.

Two structural reasons the reopening is slower than markets want to assume:

1. **Drone/asymmetry economics.** A single operator with a cheap drone or anti-ship missile can threaten a transiting tanker. The cost exchange — an expensive interceptor or platform against a ~$40k drone — is unfavorable to the defender and does not scale, even for a navy that can outspend the adversary many times over. Convoy-and-escort solutions that worked in the 1980s tanker wars were designed against a pre-drone threat.
2. **Geography.** The Iranian side of the Strait is mountainous and offers defensible, concealed firing positions; the opposite (Arabian) shore is flat and exposes fixed defenses. That asymmetry favors the side trying to keep the chokepoint contested.

If that read is right, the market's implied "back to normal in a few weeks" assumption is too optimistic, and the geopolitical risk premium in crude is more durable than the recent pullback suggests.

**Counterpoint to hold honestly:** the mid-May→June retracement shows the market is *also* capable of pricing resolution quickly. Strategic reserve releases, a credible ceasefire framework, or a faster-than-expected return of carriers could compress the premium just as violently as it expanded. The asymmetry cuts both ways.

## 3. The scenario that deserves the most attention: demand destruction

A prolonged-closure price spike is the obvious risk. The under-discussed one is **demand destruction** — a spike high enough and long enough that industrial demand simply leaves and does not return.

Two historical analogies:
- **US natural gas, 2000–01:** a spike from ~$3 to ~$10 throttled industrial activity, contributed to plant closures, and demand took years to recover.
- **European gas, 2022:** the move from ~€40/MWh toward ~€300/MWh destroyed industrial demand; a chunk of European heavy industry relocated (much of it to the US) and has not come back.

The relevant point for positioning: a demand-destruction outcome is *bad for growth and equities broadly* but does not necessarily resolve into cheap oil quickly, because the supply side stays impaired. It is the scenario with no clean hedge, which is itself an argument for owning real, cash-generative assets rather than long-duration growth.

## 4. Cross-asset read-through

**Rates.** The mechanism that matters for equity multiples is the cost of capital. Higher and more durable energy prices feed inflation and term premium; major sovereign 10-year yields (Germany, UK, Australia, Japan) have been trending higher, and the US 10-year is the anchor. Higher hurdle rates compress the present value of distant cash flows — which is precisely what makes long-duration growth/tech expensive and "here-and-now" cash flow (dividends, producing assets) relatively more attractive. *This is a conditional thesis, not a certainty: if the disruption resolves and triggers a growth scare, yields could fall instead.*

**Tech vs. energy rotation.** The structural observation is sound and worth keeping:
- Energy is **under 4% of the S&P 500** today versus ~30% in 1980. A sector that small can be ignored by institutional allocators — which is exactly the setup for an outsized move if capital does rotate, because there isn't enough float to absorb it gently.
- The AI/hyperscaler complex is **short energy and short copper** by construction. Every data center needs both. A sustained rise in power costs is a direct margin and capex headwind for the most expensively valued part of the index.

**The hyperscaler "asset-light to asset-heavy" point** is the strongest non-oil argument in the source, and it stands up:
- Hyperscaler capex is running well above internal cash generation (spending has tipped past free cash flow), and these formerly net-cash companies have moved into sizable debt issuance to fund buildouts.
- The market still pays asset-light multiples (high-20s/30x) for businesses that are starting to carry utility-like characteristics: multi-decade power purchase agreements, heavy physical infrastructure, and rising leverage. A utility is priced closer to 10–12x. If the re-rating happens, it's a multiple-compression story independent of whether oil spikes.
- Caveat: "overvalued" is not a timing signal. Concentration and high multiples can persist far longer than they "should," and AI revenue could grow into the capex. This is a multi-year thesis, not a catalyst-dated trade.

## 5. Positioning framework

The cleanest organizing lens is **asset duration**:

- **Short-duration (favored):** producing oil & gas, oilfield services, physical commodities, cash-generative real assets, dividend payers. Cash flow arrives now and is repriced upward by the supply shock.
- **Long-duration (de-emphasized / hedge against):** non-dividend growth, late-stage venture, businesses spending well beyond cash flow on the promise of distant returns.

Within energy, the source's preference for **services over producers** over a 5–10 year horizon is reasonable and supported by the setup: a genuine capex/exploration upcycle (driven by US shale plateauing *and* the supply scare) benefits the companies that get paid per active rig/tool/day, and services have structurally underperformed producers for over a decade — a wide gap that tends to mean-revert when capex turns.

Add **critical minerals / copper** as a parallel structural-short-supply theme tied to the same electrification and AI-buildout demand.

## 6. Reality check on "energy stocks defying logic"

The original claimed energy equities weren't responding — Exxon supposedly flat-to-down while Brent was sharply higher. **That snapshot has not held.** Year to date, XLE's largest holdings (Exxon, Chevron, ConocoPhillips, Williams) are each up roughly **23–28%**, and the sector has been one of the better performers of the year. Exxon and Chevron management have publicly flagged that Brent could push toward **$150–160** if inventories draw to record lows.

So the correct framing is *not* "the market refuses to believe it." It's narrower: there was a brief late-March-to-early-May window where equities lagged the crude move (the market doubted price durability), and that lag has largely closed. The live debate now is **whether the geopolitical premium persists** — i.e., whether $90s–$100s Brent is a sustained earnings base or a spike to be faded. Energy equities have repriced for "higher," not yet for "permanently much higher."

## 7. Trade structures referenced in the source (updated caveats)

These are presented as illustrative structures, not recommendations. **Critically: all specific prices below were quoted as of ~May 13 and are stale** — Brent and the underlying equities have moved since, so every strike/premium needs to be re-priced at current levels before it means anything. Position sizing was suggested small (sub-2% of portfolio) and held with patience.

1. **Oil-services exposure via deep in-the-money calls (e.g., SLB as a liquid services proxy).** The logic: a deep ITM long-dated call (e.g., Jan-2028, strike well below spot) behaves like ~2:1 geared stock with effectively no time premium to decay, and can be rolled forward at low cost if the underlying is roughly flat. Reasonable structure *if* you want leveraged services exposure and can tolerate the gearing; re-check the current premium and breakeven.
2. **Small-cap services turnarounds (e.g., Alam Maritim, ACT Energy Technologies).** Post-restructuring subsea/IRM and directional-drilling names trading at low multiples with net-cash balance sheets and decommissioning/exploration pipelines. *Liquidity is the binding constraint* — the source itself flagged Alam's daily volume as too thin for a sizable position. These are speculative, illiquid, and only appropriate as small positions for investors comfortable with the risk.
3. **NASDAQ bear put spread (QQQ) as portfolio insurance.** Framed correctly as a *hedge*, not a directional call — the concern is a liquidity squeeze where a sharp tech drawdown drags energy/commodity names down in sympathy. A long-dated, deep out-of-the-money put spread is a small-premium, high-payoff-if-it-happens structure. Treat it as insurance with an expected small loss, not an expected winner. **The private-markets stress unfolding right now (Section 8) is the most concrete candidate trigger for exactly this scenario.**

## 8. The systemic liquidity wildcard: private-credit-to-private-equity contagion

Separate from the energy thesis but directly relevant to the hedge above, a private-markets liquidity event is escalating in real time as of June 3, 2026. It matters here because it is the leading candidate to trigger the cross-asset liquidity squeeze that the QQQ hedge insures against — and because its root cause (over-exposure to software/AI-disruption-risk borrowers) rhymes with the AI-bubble concern already running through this analysis.

**What is actually happening (verified):**

- **Private credit redemptions are accelerating, not fading.** The ~$31B Cliffwater Corporate Lending Fund (CCLFX) capped Q2 redemptions at 5% after investors requested roughly **17%** — meaning redeemers get back under a third of what they asked for. That is *up* from ~14% requested (7% honored) the prior quarter. S&P moved its outlook on the fund to negative in March, citing liquidity pressure and noting roughly **25% software exposure**.
- **The same pressure has hit the largest managers.** Apollo, KKR, BlackRock (HPS), Blue Owl, and Blackstone (BCRED) have all run into elevated tender activity and 5–7% caps over recent quarters; Blackstone even deployed firm/employee capital to help meet outflows.
- **It has now jumped from credit to equity.** Today, Partners Group (~$185B AUM) gated its ~$8.6B Global Value SICAV *evergreen private equity* fund at 5% after redemption requests hit ~**9.8–10%**. Its shares fell **13–18%** (worst day since its 2006 IPO) and dragged down KKR, EQT, Blackstone, and Ares in sympathy. The credit-to-equity spillover is the genuinely new signal.

**Why the mechanics matter more than the headlines.** The useful framework here is a staged escalation:

1. *Hidden stress* — weakening borrowers, falling interest coverage, rising use of payment-in-kind (PIK) interest, loan modifications, and maturity extensions that defer rather than resolve problems.
2. *Liquidity pressure* — redemption requests exceed gates, secondary sales pick up, investors start questioning whether reported NAVs are real.
3. *Credit crunch* — ratings downgrades, tightening funding lines, repricing of collateral, and forced selling ("sell what you can, not what you want").

The reflexive risk is that **gates reveal pressure rather than eliminate it.** Before a gate, investors believe they have liquidity; after one, they know they're in a queue — which rationally accelerates further redemptions. That feeds a loop: downgrade risk → faster redemptions → forced sales → weaker price discovery → lower marks → more redemptions. This is why a private-markets bust can look calm on the surface and then move non-linearly: it cracks through gates, queues, markdowns, and ratings actions rather than through visible price crashes.

**The honest counterweight.** So far this is predominantly a *liquidity and redemption* story, not yet a wave of borrower defaults — wanting cash back is not the same as loans going bad. Partners Group says it had ample liquidity to meet the requests and chose the gate to protect long-term investors, and its credit funds (under 3% of AUM) report no net redemptions. A diversified, first-lien-heavy book can be both senior and hard to sell quickly. The market should be careful about turning one fund's queue into a systemic panic; it may stabilize.

**Read-through for positioning:** the danger to the energy/commodity thesis is not fundamental — it's correlation. In a forced-deleveraging event, almost everything sells together regardless of merit, and crowded "winners" (including energy if it has become a consensus long by then) get sold to raise cash. That is precisely the liquidity-squeeze risk the put-spread hedge is designed for, and it is the strongest current argument for carrying some protection rather than running the energy book unhedged.

## 9. What would invalidate the thesis (the bear case)

A one-sided thesis is a fragile one. The conditions that would break this:

- **A credible, durable reopening** of the Strait — carriers and insurers returning, not just political statements. The recent ~15% Brent pullback shows how fast the premium can deflate.
- **Coordinated SPR / strategic reserve releases** plus OPEC-adjacent spare capacity coming back faster than expected (e.g., Gulf producers restoring shut-in volumes within weeks of reopening).
- **Demand destruction that resolves into a deflationary growth scare** — in which case yields fall, and the "higher rates → value over growth" leg of the argument reverses even as oil stays disrupted.
- **AI revenue growing into the capex**, vindicating hyperscaler multiples and delaying or cancelling the re-rating.
- **Energy's small index weight cuts both ways:** the same thinness that could fuel a violent rotation up also means the sector can be a small, volatile, easily-ignored allocation that chops sideways for a long time.
- **A private-markets deleveraging event (Section 8)** could force broad, indiscriminate risk-off selling that hits energy and commodity longs hard in the short term — even if the underlying supply thesis is intact. The fundamentals and the price can diverge for as long as the forced selling lasts.
